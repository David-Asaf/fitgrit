Competitive Analysis and Product Requirements Document: Fitness Challenge App

Competitive Analysis: Existing Fitness & Challenge Apps in the U.S.

The U.S. market offers a variety of fitness apps with social and challenge features, ranging from major fitness platforms (e.g. Strava, Fitbit, Apple’s fitness ecosystem) to niche apps dedicated to group challenges. Below is an analysis of key competitors, focusing on their core fitness/challenge features, integration capabilities, pricing models, challenge-sharing ease, and cross-platform support.

Strava

Core Features & Social Challenges: Strava brands itself as “the social network for athletes,” allowing users to track activities (running, cycling, hiking, etc.) via GPS and share them in a feed ￼. It offers competitive features like leaderboards on segments (specific route sections) and user-created clubs where members can see each other’s workouts and compete on weekly leaderboards ￼ ￼. Strava also hosts official challenges (e.g. monthly distance or elevation challenges sponsored by brands) which users can join to earn digital badges. However, Strava’s user-generated group challenges are somewhat limited – while you can compete with friends via club leaderboards or informal comparison, the platform does not natively support custom ad-hoc challenge events for a closed group beyond club activities.

Integration: Strava is highly integrative. It can ingest data from a wide range of devices and services: users can record directly with the Strava app or sync workouts from Garmin, Fitbit, Polar, Suunto, and other trackers via linked accounts. Strava also connects with Apple Health and Google Fit – for example, Strava can read from or write workouts to these platforms (it will send GPS activity data to Google Fit and read weight data, and can sync with Apple HealthKit on iOS) ￼ ￼. Notably, Fitbit users can connect their accounts so that Fitbit-recorded activities automatically appear in Strava ￼. This broad compatibility makes Strava a central hub for fitness data aggregation.

Pricing Model: Strava operates on a freemium model. The core tracking and social features are free (including joining clubs and global challenges), but a paid subscription (~$60/year) unlocks advanced analytics, segment leaderboards, route planning, and other premium features. There are no one-time challenge fees; challenges are included in the free usage, while revenue comes from subscriptions.

Challenge Joining & Sharing: To join official Strava challenges, users simply tap to join in-app. For club competitions, a user must join the club (which can be public or private). Sharing a challenge externally is not a primary Strava feature – you typically join via the app interface (or a link to a Strava club invite). There isn’t a single “invite link” for a custom challenge because Strava’s model revolves around following friends or joining clubs, rather than one-off challenge links. In other words, ease of joining group challenges on Strava depends on already having a network on the app (you might invite friends to a club or to follow you, but not a standalone challenge link).

Cross-Platform Support: Strava is fully cross-platform. It has robust native apps on iOS and Android, and a rich web application. Data syncs across all platforms in real time. This ubiquity, plus integration with wearable devices, has contributed to Strava’s popularity among diverse user groups ￼.

Fitbit (Mobile App & Ecosystem)

Core Features & Social Challenges: The Fitbit app is designed to work with Fitbit’s wearable devices (trackers and smartwatches), though it can also use a smartphone’s sensors for basic tracking if no device is present ￼. It automatically logs steps, distance, active minutes, sleep, etc. from the wearable. Fitbit has a strong social component: users can add friends on the platform and compete in challenges. The app includes built-in group challenges such as Daily Showdown (most steps in a day), Workweek Hustle (steps over the work week), Weekend Warrior, and Goal Challenges, as well as themed adventures. These challenges allow up to 10 friends to compete on a shared leaderboard for step count or active minutes ￼. The social feed also lets users cheer or taunt friends. Overall, Fitbit focuses on friendly competition among known friends/family. However, its challenge scope is somewhat limited to Fitbit’s predefined formats.

Integration: Fitbit’s ecosystem is more closed. The Fitbit app can sync its data to Apple Health or other services only in limited ways (for example, Fitbit does not natively write to Apple HealthKit; users often rely on third-party solutions to sync data). Direct integrations include linking Fitbit to Strava (Fitbit activities can appear on Strava) ￼, and Fitbit can share steps data with some partner apps (e.g. MyFitnessPal for calories). The Fitbit API allows third-party apps (including corporate wellness platforms or niche challenges) to pull data with user permission, which some challenge apps utilize ￼. Notably, Fitbit does not integrate with Google Fit (since it effectively serves a similar purpose on Android). After Google’s acquisition of Fitbit, there are efforts to integrate with Google’s Health Connect on Android, but as of now Fitbit data largely stays within Fitbit’s app unless manually shared out.

Pricing Model: The Fitbit app is free with the device purchase, and all the standard tracking and challenge features are free to use. Fitbit monetizes via device sales and Fitbit Premium (a subscription ~$10/month that adds advanced wellness reports, readiness scores, video workouts, etc.). Challenges and basic social features are included in the free tier ￼. There is no concept of paying per challenge – any Fitbit owner can participate in challenges freely, though the device cost is an upfront barrier and some advanced metrics require Premium.

Challenge Joining & Sharing: To join a challenge on Fitbit, one typically must be friends with the inviter in the Fitbit app. The challenge invites are sent in-app (with notifications) or via email to existing Fitbit contacts. Joining via link is not a standard flow – you can’t simply click a web link to join a Fitbit challenge unless you already have the app and an invite through your account. Thus, ease of inviting is moderate: it’s easy to start a challenge with your Fitbit friends, but not as straightforward to include new users who aren’t on Fitbit. Fitbit does not have deep-link invitation for challenges; it relies on its internal social graph.

Cross-Platform Support: The Fitbit app is available on iOS and Android (and there’s a web dashboard for data). It supports syncing with its devices across platforms equally. However, it is effectively limited to those who own Fitbit hardware or carry their phone for step counting. Unlike some apps, Fitbit’s social competition can’t include someone who uses a Garmin or Apple Watch – everyone in a Fitbit challenge needs to log data via Fitbit’s ecosystem (phone app or device).

Apple Fitness / Apple Watch (Activity Sharing)

Core Features & Challenges: Apple’s fitness features revolve around the Activity Rings (Move, Exercise, Stand) tracked by the Apple Watch and the Fitness app on iPhone. Users can share their activity with friends and engage in one-on-one challenges: Apple allows you to competitively compare your daily activity with a friend over a 7-day competition, where each person earns points for filling their rings, and at week’s end a winner is declared. This is a very engaging feature for many Apple Watch users to spur each other on ￼. However, Apple’s system only supports 1v1 challenges, not group challenges – you can have many friends and see each other’s progress, but official competitions are limited to two participants at a time. For broader social motivation, Apple’s Fitness app includes features like sharing workouts completed, sending cheers or smack talk, and seeing a friends ranking of calories burned for the week, but no multi-person challenge event by default.

Integration: Apple’s approach is device-centric. The Apple HealthKit platform on iOS aggregates data from the Apple Watch and other connected apps. Apple’s Fitness data can be shared with third-party apps through HealthKit read/write permissions. For instance, third-party challenge apps (like Stridekick or Challenges app) often pull data from Apple Health to get a user’s steps or workouts. Apple’s Fitness app itself doesn’t integrate outward to other tracking services (no direct sync to Strava or Fitbit; instead those apps integrate into Apple Health if they choose). In summary, Apple provides a hub (Health app) that many apps can draw from, but if you’re strictly using Apple’s Fitness app, you’re mostly in their walled garden. Apple does not support Google Fit obviously, and cross-platform use is not applicable (no Apple Fitness on Android).

Pricing Model: The Fitness app and activity sharing are free with Apple hardware. There’s no fee to use the activity features or compete with friends – one just needs an Apple Watch (not cheap, but that’s the hardware model). Apple Fitness+ is a separate subscription service for guided workouts, but that’s not directly related to group challenge tracking. So for challenges, there are no subscriptions or payments in Apple’s model beyond owning their devices.

Challenge Joining & Sharing: Inviting a friend to Apple’s 7-day challenge is done through the Apple Watch/iPhone interface by selecting a friend (who must also have an Apple Watch) and inviting them. It’s seamless if both parties are on Apple’s platform, but not shareable via web link or to non-Apple users. Essentially, Apple’s challenge feature is tightly bound to its ecosystem and relies on the user’s contact list (or Apple ID friends) – you cannot involve an Android user at all. The ease of joining is high for Apple users (simple tap to accept a challenge on your watch/iPhone), and non-existent for anyone outside iOS.

Cross-Platform Support: iOS only. There is no Android or web version of Apple’s Fitness app or Activity sharing – it’s exclusively for iPhone/Apple Watch users ￼. This exclusivity is a major limitation in mixed groups.

Garmin Connect

Core Features & Social Challenges: Garmin’s ecosystem (Garmin Connect app and website) serves users of Garmin wearables and GPS devices. It provides detailed tracking for many activities (running, cycling, swimming, etc.) and health metrics. Socially, Garmin allows users to add “Connections” (friends) and join groups. The platform offers Challenges: Garmin regularly posts monthly challenges (e.g. run 5K, walk certain steps) that users can join for badges. More pertinent to group competition, Garmin Connect lets users create Group Challenges among their connections. For example, a user can set up a step challenge for the week and invite specific friends to compete – the app will then show a leaderboard among those people ￼ ￼. This requires creating a group or having connections in Garmin; once set up, participants see a private leaderboard updated with their device data. It’s a useful feature for Garmin-owning friend groups, though it may not be as flexible or prominent as dedicated challenge apps.

Integration: Garmin Connect is moderately open: users can link Garmin to third-party services. Many athletes sync their Garmin activities to Strava (via a direct Garmin-Strava link), to MyFitnessPal (for calorie tracking), or other apps. Garmin’s platform itself can ingest data from certain sources (e.g. it can read steps from Apple Health via the Connect iOS app if granted, and on Android Garmin can use Health Connect to share data). Garmin does not integrate with Fitbit (competitor) or Apple Watch (different ecosystem), but it can export data or be linked to Apple HealthKit and Google’s Health Connect for basic data sharing. Many challenge platforms (like Challenge Hound or Stridekick) allow users to connect their Garmin account to pull activity data ￼. In short, Garmin is willing to share data with partner apps through APIs and linking, making it possible for cross-platform challenge apps to include Garmin users.

Pricing Model: The Garmin Connect app is free for anyone with a Garmin device (or even without one, it can use phone sensors for basic tracking). There is no subscription for using Garmin Connect’s core features. Garmin makes money from device sales rather than app fees. Challenges and social features are included for free. There is a Garmin premium service (Garmin Coach training plans, etc.) but not related to challenges. So, similar to Fitbit, there’s no per-challenge charge – it’s part of the product experience.

Challenge Joining & Sharing: To join a friend’s challenge on Garmin, you must be connected as friends (connections) on Garmin Connect. The organizer creates a challenge (via the app or web) and selects which connections or group to include ￼ ￼. Invitations show up via app notification or email. There isn’t a public link that outsiders can click to join; it’s contained in the Garmin Connect system. The ease of joining is reasonable for Garmin users (just accept the invite in-app), but like Fitbit/Apple it’s closed to that ecosystem. Garmin does have an expedition mode where users can join community challenges or virtual events via codes, but those are more for global events and still require a Garmin account. Cross-group sharing via simple link is not a primary flow here.

Cross-Platform Support: Garmin Connect is offered on iOS and Android, and also accessible via web. The experience and features are similar on both mobile platforms (with integration to each platform’s health APIs as noted). Garmin’s cross-platform support is good, but again, only Garmin device owners typically use it – it’s not intended for someone without Garmin hardware except for very limited use.

Google Fit

Core Features & Social Challenges: Google Fit is Google’s health tracking app, providing a dashboard for physical activity (steps, active minutes, heart points) using phone sensors or Wear OS smartwatches. Its focus is personal health goals rather than competitions. Notably, Google Fit does not have built-in social or group challenge features – there’s no native friend list or challenge mechanism in the app. It’s more of a personal journal. Some third-party Android apps layer on challenges by reading from Google Fit, but within Google Fit itself, you won’t find friend leaderboards or group challenges.

Integration: The strength of Google Fit lies in integration. It acts as a central hub on Android: many fitness apps can read/write to Google Fit (steps, workouts, heart rate, etc.) ￼. For instance, an app like Stridekick or Challenges can use Google Fit to get your step count from any Android phone or Wear watch. Google Fit also exists on iOS (as a standalone app that syncs with Apple Health data to some degree), mainly to allow Google-centric users to collect data on an iPhone. Google’s newer Health Connect (on Android) further facilitates sharing data between apps. So while Google Fit isn’t a competitive app itself, it’s an integration point used by many challenge apps to gather activity from users who might not have a specific tracker device.

Pricing Model: Google Fit is completely free (no ads, no premium tier). It’s essentially a utility app and part of Google’s platform services.

Challenge Joining & Sharing: Not applicable, as Google Fit doesn’t host challenges on its own. Any sharing would be via third-party apps that use Fit data.

Cross-Platform Support: Android (and limited iOS). The primary use is on Android phones or Wear OS watches. An iOS version exists, but since most iOS users will use Apple Health, Google Fit on iOS is not commonly used for challenges. For cross-platform challenge apps, Google Fit on Android and Apple Health on iOS serve analogous roles as data sources ￼ ￼.

Nike Run Club / Nike Training Club

(Major fitness brand apps, included for context though not solely challenge-focused.)

Nike Run Club (NRC) and Nike Training Club (NTC) are popular apps for run tracking and guided workouts respectively. Core features: NRC tracks runs (pace, distance, GPS route) and has a strong social component of community and coaching. Users can follow friends and see a leaderboard of total miles run among friends each month, for example. Nike also features occasional global challenges (like “Run 50k this month” with achievement badges). However, user-created group challenges are not a primary feature – you cannot create a custom challenge with specific friends inside NRC; you mainly compare progress through the friend leaderboard or join Nike’s official challenges. NTC is more about workout videos and has limited social features (it might allow sharing workout completion or seeing friends’ activities if connected).

Integration: Nike’s apps can sync with Apple Health (NRC runs can be written to HealthKit) and can import data from Apple Watch. NRC used to integrate with Strava by allowing you to share runs to Strava (earlier via an Apple Health workaround or IFTTT, but no direct link currently except manual export). There’s no integration with Google Fit directly from Nike’s side, though on Android NRC likely uses Google Fit services for step counting. Nike apps don’t integrate with Garmin or Fitbit – they are more standalone.

Pricing: Both NRC and NTC are free apps with no subscription (Nike removed the subscription from NTC in recent years, offering workouts free). They act as brand engagement tools for Nike. Challenges or any social features come at no cost.

Platforms: NRC and NTC are available on iOS and Android. They offer a fairly uniform experience on both. There’s no web version for participating – all done via mobile.

Challenges and Sharing: Within NRC, joining an official challenge is just a tap (and often these are open to all users globally). For friend leaderboards, you simply need to add friends in the app. There isn’t a link-based sharing for individual challenges since you can’t create a bespoke challenge. Nike’s approach is less flexible for custom group competitions compared to others, focusing instead on global community challenges and basic friend comparisons.

Pacer Pedometer & Step Challenges

Core Features: Pacer is a popular mobile app (100M+ downloads) that turns your phone into a step counter and also supports wearables like Apple Watch ￼ ￼. Pacer provides automatic step tracking, GPS walk tracking, and wellness coaching features. Importantly, it has a strong social challenge component. Users can join public group challenges or create private challenges with friends. Pacer supports various challenge types – for example, you can set up individual or group step challenges based on total steps, distance covered, or even virtual “race” goals ￼. You can define the duration (e.g. a week, a month) and invite participants. Everyone’s progress is updated in real time on a leaderboard ￼. Pacer also offers virtual adventure challenges (often with digital medals or even purchasable medals) for motivation ￼. The app combines being a personal activity tracker with being a challenges platform.

Integration: Pacer primarily uses your phone’s sensors and connected apps. On iOS it integrates with Apple HealthKit to import data (so it can pull steps from an Apple Watch, for instance) ￼. On Android, it likely uses Google Fit or the phone’s built-in step counter. Pacer does not directly integrate with Fitbit or Garmin through their APIs – instead, it focuses on phone and Apple Watch data. (Users with other devices could link them to Apple Health or Google Fit to indirectly get data into Pacer.) Pacer’s aim is to be device-agnostic by leveraging the smartphone as the common denominator.

Pricing Model: The base app is free with advertising and basic features ￼. Pacer also has a Premium subscription (~$5/month) that unlocks advanced features: deeper analytics, certain training plans, and some advanced challenge features (like specialized challenges or more slots) ￼. In the free version, you can still join and create standard challenges with friends or join community challenges. Pacer monetizes via premium and also by selling virtual challenge medals and corporate wellness services ￼.

Challenge Joining & Sharing: Pacer makes it easy to join challenges. Public challenges (like “30-day step challenge”) are discoverable in-app for anyone. For private challenges, you can invite friends via a shareable link or code – when they click the link, it directs them to download Pacer and join the specific challenge (Pacer’s support documentation mentions creating a group and sharing a code/link to invite others) ￼. This lowers the friction for bringing new people into a challenge. The app also has community groups and even supports corporate challenge portals, indicating a focus on easy onboarding. Cross-platform is not an issue since Pacer is on both major mobile OS. One limitation: those without a smartphone (or unwilling to use one for step tracking) can’t easily participate, as there’s no web tracking interface for individuals (the web is only for admins in corporate use).

Cross-Platform Support: Pacer is available on iOS and Android ￼. The experience is similar on both; data syncs through the user’s account so an iPhone user and an Android user can compete in the same challenge. There is no dedicated web app for end-users, but Pacer does have a web portal for its Workplace version. Overall, it’s effectively cross-platform among mobile users.

Stridekick

Core Features: Stridekick is an app entirely focused on step challenges and activity competitions. It’s known for allowing people with different fitness trackers or smartphones to compete together. In Stridekick, a user can create a challenge (various types like total steps, daily average, streaks, etc.) and invite friends. It supports challenges with unlimited participants in certain modes (popular for workplaces or large groups) ￼ ￼. Core features include real-time leaderboards, challenge chat for banter, and the ability to join public group challenges. Stridekick provides an engaging UI with badges and progress bars to keep motivation high. It’s more bare-bones than something like Pacer in terms of tracking (Stridekick doesn’t track workouts beyond steps/active minutes without an external device), but it excels at the social competition aspect.

Integration: A major strength of Stridekick is device integration. “No fitness tracker? No problem,” the app proclaims – you can use your smartphone’s step sensor or manually enter steps ￼. If you do have a tracker, Stridekick connects to many: it can link with Apple Health (thereby any device synced to Health like Apple Watch), Google Fit, Fitbit, Garmin, and others ￼. Essentially, a user authorizes Stridekick to read their step count or workout data from one of these sources, and Stridekick normalizes it for the challenges. This cross-compatibility is its key selling point: friends don’t need the same device or platform to compete. For example, an Apple Watch user, a Fitbit user, and a person with just a smartphone can all join the same step challenge on Stridekick.

Pricing Model: Stridekick is free for standard use – users can join and create basic challenges without payment. The company monetizes through enterprise offerings and possibly premium tiers for large events. Notably, Stridekick’s free app has a participant limit for custom challenges (the Reddit community notes that it’s ideal for “10 people or less” in a free challenge) ￼. For bigger groups or corporate challenges, Stridekick offers paid solutions (e.g. a paid “Group Events” service) ￼. These are one-time or contract-based fees rather than subscriptions per user. There is no recurring subscription for individual users; monetization is likely via organizations or possibly in-app purchases for special challenge modes.

Challenge Joining & Sharing: Stridekick allows sharing challenge invite codes/links so that anyone with the app (or who downloads the app) can join the specific challenge. This makes it straightforward to get friends on board – you create a challenge and send them the invite link. The platform does not require existing social connections; you can effectively run a challenge for a group by distributing a code. Additionally, Stridekick has an option to run public challenges that anyone in the app can join, which can be shared as well. The emphasis is on inclusivity and ease of access to the challenge group.

Cross-Platform Support: Stridekick is on iOS and Android ￼ and has a web dashboard for challenge organizers. The mobile apps sync with the backend so all participants’ data (from whatever source device) updates the common leaderboard. Cross-platform operation is a core aspect – it doesn’t matter if you use Android or iPhone, as long as you can connect a step source, you can compete together. This universality is a big competitive advantage over device-specific platforms.

“Challenges – Compete, Get Fit” (FitNow)

Core Features: The Challenges app by FitNow, Inc. (makers of LoseIt!) is a mobile app dedicated to fitness competitions with friends, with an emphasis on two categories: Move More challenges (step count based) and Eat Well challenges (which combine nutrition logging with exercise) ￼. Users can join either Team Throwdowns (team vs team competitions, typically teams of 4) or Solo Smackdowns (individuals competing on a leaderboard) ￼. The app awards points for activities and even healthy eating, and converts points into Bronze, Silver, Gold medals within the challenge ￼. It also incorporates fun features like “nudges” – you can send encouragement or trash-talk to friends – and a challenge wall for comments, mimicking a social feed inside each challenge ￼. Essentially, it’s focused on short-term contests that make fitness more engaging, with both exercise and diet aspects (the latter is somewhat unique among these apps).

Integration: Challenges app integrates with Google Fit and Fitbit to automatically retrieve step and exercise data ￼. Notably, it mentions more platforms “coming soon,” and since it’s on iOS as well, it likely integrates with Apple HealthKit on iOS (allowing use with Apple Watch, etc.) – although the Android description explicitly calls out Google Fit/Fitbit. By connecting to these services, the app can auto-log a user’s steps and possibly active minutes toward the challenge goals, so manual entry isn’t needed. There is no direct mention of Garmin integration; Garmin users might have to link through Fitbit or just use another method (this could be a limitation for those users). The focus is clearly on common trackers and phone data to cast a wide net.

Pricing Model: The Challenges app itself is free to download and use, with no subscription required. FitNow’s business likely ties into their other apps or driving engagement for their ecosystem. There may be in-app purchases or ads, but the core ability to create and join challenges is free as per the app description. We didn’t find evidence of a premium tier; the app likely aims for maximum user acquisition (possibly to funnel users to LoseIt! or other services). In the current competitive landscape, offering these features for free (with maybe optional purchases like cosmetic upgrades) is plausible.

Challenge Joining & Sharing: The app supports inviting friends via email or text with a link to join your team or challenge ￼. This implies an easy onboarding flow – e.g. you send a link, they click and are guided to install the app and join the specific challenge or team. The existence of team vs team mode suggests one can create a challenge, form a team of 4 and others do the same via invites, and then the competition is on. The social wall and nudges features indicate the app is designed for engagement and ease of use among friends, even those who aren’t avid athletes. The ability to invite by link/email is a strong point for lowering friction.

Cross-Platform Support: Challenges is available on iOS and Android (it’s listed on both app stores). It relies on each OS’s health integration (Fitbit, Google Fit, Apple Health) to pull data. Users on both platforms can compete together as long as they all have the app. This cross-platform reach is necessary given it targets friend groups irrespective of phone type.

GymRats (Group Fitness Challenge app)

Core Features: GymRats is a mobile app designed for group accountability and flexible fitness challenges. Instead of automatically counting steps or minutes, GymRats centers around logging workouts in a shared group. Users create or join a group challenge and then post each workout they do (with a photo and description) to the group feed ￼ ￼. The app is “free form” – groups can define their own rules or scoring. For example, you can agree that any workout counts as 1 point, or decide to track miles or calories; the app lets you choose the scoring metric (workouts, miles, minutes, calories, steps, or even a custom point system) ￼ ￼. A leaderboard is then generated accordingly. This flexibility means GymRats can support many types of challenges (from “most gym visits” to “most calories burned” or a custom challenge like a mix of activities). It also serves simply as a shared workout log for accountability if you don’t care about competitive scoring ￼. There’s a social aspect in that the group sees each other’s workout posts (with photos or screenshots), which adds motivation and verification.

Integration: GymRats can integrate with Apple HealthKit to automatically import workouts recorded on Apple Watch or saved on the iPhone ￼. This means that instead of manually logging, a user can opt to have their runs, bike rides, etc. (as recorded by Apple’s Workout app or other Health-connected apps) auto-post to the group. This is convenient for accuracy and effort tracking. However, Apple Health integration implies GymRats is currently iOS-focused. There’s no mention of direct integration with Google Fit or other trackers in the description. Likely, at the time of writing, GymRats is an iOS-only app (with no Android version publicly noted), which would limit cross-platform usage. It may rely on manual entry on other platforms, but since it’s not available on Android, non-iOS users cannot really participate unless the app expands.

Pricing Model: GymRats is free to download and use, with the description not indicating any required payment (it even touts that it’s 4+ rated and offers in-app purchases, which might be cosmetic or donation-based). The core functionality appears free. The developers might use in-app purchases for premium features or rely on a growing user base to monetize later. For now, cost is not a barrier – any group can start using it without a subscription or fees per challenge.

Challenge Joining & Sharing: The flow in GymRats is straightforward: create a group challenge and invite as many people as you’d like ￼ ￼. Invitations likely occur via a link or code (commonly, one person creates the group and others join by searching the group name or via invite link). Since it’s free-form, you don’t need everyone to connect a device; even a simple photo and note can count as a “workout”. This makes it inclusive – someone without a tracker can still participate by manually logging their activity and a photo. GymRats essentially provides the scaffolding (feed + leaderboard), and the group defines how to use it. Sharing the challenge is as easy as getting friends to download the app and search or tap the invite link. The app’s design as per the App Store screenshots and description emphasizes simplicity and flexibility, which appeals to groups that might find device-based tracking challenges too restrictive.

Cross-Platform Support: Currently, iOS only (Designed for iPhone) ￼. As mentioned, this is a limiting factor. If all group members have iPhones, it’s great. But mixed-platform friend groups can’t use GymRats until an Android version is released. (There’s no evidence of an Android app at this time.) This is a key difference vs. some other niche apps that launched on both platforms.

Strive (Strive Competitions app)

Core Features: Strive is a mobile app focused on health & fitness competitions among friends, family, or coworkers. It supports seven competition types: Steps, Distance (Miles), Move Calories (as measured by devices), Exercise Minutes, Weight loss, Body Fat percentage, and BMI improvement ￼ ￼. Users on Strive can create a challenge selecting one of these metrics and a duration (e.g. a 30-day weight loss % challenge or a 10-week steps challenge). The app then tracks each participant’s progress relative to their baseline (for weight/BMI/body fat) or in absolute terms (for steps, miles, etc.) and displays a leaderboard ￼ ￼. The screenshots show that Strive provides a polished leaderboard view and progress charts for each user. It also allows reviewing activity and measurements over time, acting as an ongoing fitness tracker in addition to challenge scorekeeper ￼ ￼. The focus is on competition as motivation – their tagline encourages inviting others who share similar goals and “Strive to do better” together ￼.

Integration: Strive mentions that it “gets all my steps from my Apple Watch” in a testimonial ￼, indicating it integrates with Apple Health to pull data (for steps, calories, etc.). It likely also can connect with Fitbit or Google Fit, though the site explicitly references Apple Watch. As a newer app, Strive may have started on iOS first (as suggested by linking only to the Apple App Store) and thus leveraged HealthKit integration. If an Android app exists or is planned, it would presumably use Google Health Connect/Fit for data. For metrics like weight and body fat, Strive might rely on manual entry or data from smart scales synced to HealthKit. No explicit list of integrations is given on their marketing site beyond implying support for popular devices (Apple Watch at least). Given the competition types, integration with wearables (Apple, Fitbit, Garmin for steps/calories) would be essential for ease of use, otherwise users have to input data manually.

Pricing Model: Strive is free to download; there’s no mention of cost to create challenges. It’s possible Strive could introduce a premium tier for advanced analytics or larger groups in the future, but as of now the focus is on growth. They highlight use cases like office challenges, implying they might target corporate wellness sponsorships or something similar for monetization later. For the average user, it seems free to compete in any number of challenges (with the only “cost” being that some features might still be under development as it’s a young app).

Challenge Joining & Sharing: Creating a challenge in Strive is straightforward: pick metric and duration, then invite friends via the app. The app likely allows inviting contacts through a link or code; their website says “invite friends, family, coworkers” without friction ￼. Since Strive supports relatively large groups (scaling to possibly hundreds or more, given “unlimited users” is hinted on their site for events) ￼, sharing a join link is the practical method. The app does not seem to have a public community feed – it’s about private competitions – so you share directly with those you want in. The user experience is built around seeing a clear leaderboard and each person’s stats (for example, in a weight loss % challenge, everyone’s percentage change is shown). This clarity and the variety of competition metrics make it appealing for serious fitness groups.

Cross-Platform Support: Currently, iOS-only (the download link provided is for the App Store, and no Android link is mentioned on their site) ￼. This suggests at this moment, all participants need an iPhone (and ideally an Apple Watch for automatic data) to fully utilize Strive. An Android version may be in development, but lack of cross-platform out of the gate is a competitive disadvantage for reaching wider audiences. If/when they expand to Android, using Google’s Health Connect will be critical to pull in step and workout data similarly.

StepBet (DietBet/StepBet by WayBetter)

Core Features: StepBet is a unique entry in this space – it turns fitness challenges into a game with real money at stake. In StepBet, users join a step challenge “game” that typically lasts 6 weeks. Each user has personalized step targets (based on their historical activity, often using a baseline from their tracker) and must meet those goals every week. Everyone puts in an entry fee (bet), say $40. Those who successfully hit their step goals for all weeks “win” and split the pot (earning back their bet plus a small profit), whereas those who fail to meet the goals forfeit their bet ￼ ￼. It’s essentially a behavioral motivation platform, using money as incentive. There are public games anyone can join and also private games for groups of friends or corporate wellness programs. The focus is less on direct competition (there’s no leaderboard for highest steps; it’s more you vs. your goal) and more on accountability – but it’s still a form of challenge.

Integration: StepBet works with all major fitness tracking sources to automatically verify step counts. Supported integrations include Garmin, Apple Health (which covers Apple Watch), Google Fit (for Android), and Fitbit ￼ ￼. Essentially, a user connects whichever device/app they use to StepBet, and the service reads step data to ensure goals are met. If someone only has a Samsung or other phone, the Google Fit integration via Health Connect can pull steps from any Android phone or Wear OS watch ￼ ￼. This broad integration is necessary for fairness and accuracy since money is involved – StepBet independently validates your activity through these connections.

Pricing Model: Participation in StepBet requires a monetary bet. The app itself is free to download, but each game has an entry fee (usually $20-$50) which goes into the prize pot. The company (WayBetter) takes a cut of the pot for operating the game (unless you are a StepBet Member with a subscription, in which case the cut is smaller and you can play in more games simultaneously). The Member subscription is optional ($50/year) for heavy users. So the pricing model is pay-per-challenge, but unlike a simple fee, it’s a bet you can win back. This is quite different from other apps; it’s a gambling/motivation hybrid model. There are no one-time purchase for features – the one-time payments are the game fees, which vary by challenge and group size (pots grow with more people, but your fee is fixed).

Challenge Joining & Sharing: To join a StepBet, users typically find games in the app or via a link. For private games, an organizer can share an invite code or link to their friends. The join process includes paying the bet via in-app purchase. Because real money is used, the onboarding flow is a bit more involved (account setup, payment, and connecting your tracker must all be done). However, StepBet provides a clear guide and even a preparation week before the challenge officially starts, to ensure everyone’s data is syncing correctly. The competitive aspect isn’t head-to-head steps but rather mutual encouragement to not lose the bet – all players who meet goals win together (“win together with StepBet” is their tagline) ￼. Sharing the concept to new users might require explanation, but the app handles verifying activity so users just focus on hitting daily step goals.

Cross-Platform Support: StepBet is on iOS and Android. It fully supports cross-platform competitions since everything is synced to their server. An iPhone user with Apple Watch and an Android user with Fitbit can join the same game without issue. The integrations cover the platform differences (Apple Health vs Google Fit). The app’s user experience is similar on both platforms.

Challenge Hound (Web & Mobile Platform)

Core Features: Challenge Hound allows creating highly customizable virtual challenges and races for groups ￼ ￼. Basically, Challenge Hound is built to handle complex rules and large participant numbers, appealing to corporations, nonprofits, and serious challenge organizers ￼ ￼. It’s a robust platform for those needing more than a casual step contest.

Integration: Challenge Hound boasts integration with many apps and devices. It can automatically sync activities from Garmin Connect, Fitbit, Strava, and the Under Armour/MapMyFitness apps (MapMyRun/Ride/Walk/Fitness) ￼. Pricing Model: Challenge Hound offers a free trial for personal use, but for larger or longer-term use it operates on a subscription or event-fee model. It’s often used by organizations that pay for hosting a challenge (for example, a company might pay a fee to run a wellness challenge for employees). Individuals can use it free for small casual groups with limited features, but advanced features (custom branding, very large groups) and certain challenge types might require a paid plan. The exact pricing isn’t publicly listed, as it likely depends on the size and needs (the wording on their site suggests contacting sales for big groups). In essence, it’s not a simple consumer app with in-app purchases; it’s positioned as a platform/partner for challenges ￼. For the context of our analysis, Challenge Hound highlights the high end of challenge platforms (enterprise-level features).

Challenge Joining & Sharing: Typically, an organizer creates a challenge on the web dashboard and then invites participants via a signup link. Users sign up on the Challenge Hound site or app and join the challenge (often with a code or link provided). Since it supports thousands of participants, it’s designed for self-service joining – the invite link is the primary way, possibly with email invitations as well. The participant experience, once joined, is to view the shared leaderboard/progress (they have a web view and presumably in-app view). The platform also supports adding friends or “following” others, but the main mode is one-time challenge participation rather than ongoing social feed.

Cross-Platform Support: Challenge Hound is available on Web, iOS, and Android ￼. The mobile apps likely serve as companions to the web platform. All participants can use whichever interface is convenient; data syncing and leaderboards are centralized in the cloud. This ensures truly cross-platform participation – even those without smartphones can interact via web browser. The flexibility here is maximal, aligning with their goal to accommodate any and all users in a challenge.

Summary of Key Findings: Existing fitness apps with challenge features vary in their approach:
	•	Core Features: Major fitness apps (Strava, Fitbit, Garmin, Apple) integrate challenges as a subset of broader tracking and social networking features, often focusing on simple metrics like steps or workout count among friends ￼ ￼. Niche apps (Stridekick, Challenges, GymRats, etc.) center their entire experience on the challenge mechanics, offering richer customization (team vs team, custom scoring, etc.) ￼ ￼. Some platforms incorporate unique angles (diet tracking in Challenges app, monetary incentives in StepBet).
	•	Integrations: The ability to sync with Apple Health, Google Fit, Fitbit, Garmin, etc. is a critical differentiator. Niche cross-platform apps like Stridekick and Challenge Hound excel here, connecting to virtually any data source to include all users ￼ ￼. Major single-brand apps often limit to their own devices (Fitbit, Garmin) or OS (Apple), though they may connect outwards to a few services (e.g. Fitbit->Strava). For a new entrant, supporting HealthKit and Google’s fitness APIs (which cover most devices through proxies) will be essential.
	•	Pricing Models: Subscriptions vs one-time fees: Most consumer fitness apps use freemium models with optional subscriptions (Strava, Fitbit Premium, Pacer Premium) ￼. Device-centric apps bake cost into hardware. Niche challenge apps often try to remain free for small groups to encourage growth, charging only for larger groups or special features (Stridekick, Challenge Hound) ￼ ￼. A few have novel monetization like StepBet’s betting system. Notably, none of the major players charge a one-time fee per challenge event in a straightforward way – that concept (charging per challenge) is relatively uncommon, aside from event-based platforms or StepBet’s pooled entry fees.
	•	Joining & Sharing Ease: There’s a trend toward deep linking invites for challenges in newer apps: for example, Challenges app and Stridekick allow sending a link/code to friends so they can join a specific challenge easily ￼. Older ecosystems (Fitbit, Garmin) rely on internal friend lists and do not provide one-click external invites – this can be a hassle if not everyone is on the platform. Ease of onboarding is crucial for viral growth in challenge-focused apps.
	•	Cross-Platform Support: Being on both iOS and Android is essentially required for a broad consumer app (Stridekick, Pacer, StepBet all knew this). Some excellent apps (GymRats, Strive) started iOS-only, which likely limits their reach to close-knit groups of iPhone users. A truly cross-platform approach, possibly with a web interface as well, yields the widest accessibility and addresses mixed-device audiences ￼.

Apps like Strive and others provide clear leaderboards, showing each participant’s progress and rank in the challenge. This kind of interface is common across many challenge-focused apps – it keeps users engaged by tracking who is in the lead and how far others have progressed ￼ ￼.

Implication for New App: There’s an opportunity for a cross-platform fitness challenge app that learns from these findings – combining broad integration (like Stridekick/ChallengeHound) with a polished, challenge-centric user experience (like Challenges or Strive) and a novel monetization (perhaps the per-challenge fee idea). The new app can fill gaps like enabling very large group challenges (thousands of users) which many friend-focused apps don’t handle, and doing so without requiring subscriptions, which appeals to users wary of yet another monthly fee. The competitive landscape shows demand for flexibility (different challenge types, team options), ease of use (invite links, auto-sync of workouts), and inclusivity (no device left behind). Product Requirements Document: “FitGrit” (Fitness Challenge App)

Product Vision: FitGrit is a cross-platform mobile application (iOS & Android) built with React Native (Expo) and Supabase, dedicated entirely to fitness challenges. It enables users to seamlessly create, join, and participate in fitness challenges with groups of any size – from a close circle of friends to a whole community. Unlike traditional fitness apps that emphasize solo tracking or social feeds, FitGrit’s core experience is the challenge itself. It brings people together around goal-oriented fitness competitions, integrating with popular fitness data sources so everyone’s progress is counted. The monetization eschews subscriptions in favor of a simple one-time fee per challenge (scaling by group size), making it easy to start a challenge on a whim or for special events without a recurring cost burden. Our mission is to make group fitness challenges fun, inclusive, and effortless, thereby motivating users to stay active through friendly competition.

Target Users and Archetypes

To ensure we build the right features first, we consider a few user archetypes:
	•	The Motivated Friend Group (“Social Exercisers”) – e.g. Alice, who exercises casually, and her 5 friends spread across iOS and Android with various devices. They want a simple way to do a monthly fitness challenge (like “most steps in November” or “workout 5 days a week”) to keep each other accountable. They value ease of use (quick setup, minimal tech hassle) and fun competition (chat, leaderboards, maybe a prize pot they chip in for charity or reward).
	•	The Fitness Enthusiast (“Data-driven Competitor”) – e.g. Brian, who uses a Garmin watch and participates in online fitness communities. He wants to host large challenges (say a global 1000-person mileage challenge or a local running club’s weekly competition). He cares about integration (so his and others’ workouts from Garmin/Strava/Apple all count), scalability (the app should handle many participants), and advanced challenge options (team competitions, multi-metric scoring). He’s willing to pay a small fee to host big events but doesn’t want subscriptions since his usage is event-based.
	•	The Corporate Wellness Organizer (“Team Builder”) – e.g. Claire, an HR specialist organizing a company-wide fitness challenge for 200 employees. She needs an app that everyone can use regardless of phone or wearable, with simple onboarding (easy invites), team support (departments competing), and maybe customization (company branding or custom challenge rules). Cost needs to be predictable (one-time per challenge fits budgeting better than many individual app subscriptions).
	•	The Individual Achiever (“Solo Challenger”) – e.g. Dan, who might join public challenges to stay motivated even if his friends aren’t involved. He’s looking for variety of challenges he can join via a link or in-app list, and community aspects like seeing his rank among many. While not central to early adoption, this user keeps engagement high by always joining something.

These archetypes inform prioritization: initially, we’ll focus on small friend groups and fitness enthusiasts (for organic growth and feedback). Later, we ensure the platform scales for large use cases (corporate and community challenges).

Core Features and Functionality

1. Challenge Creation & Management
	•	Create Challenge (MVP): Users can create a new fitness challenge in a few taps. They choose:
	•	Challenge Type: e.g. Total Steps, Active Minutes, Workout Count, Distance (km/mi), Calories Burned, or Specific Activity (like Cycling Distance, Swimming Time, etc.). Initially support a core set of 4–5 types (e.g. Steps, Workout Minutes, Distance, Workouts Count) which covers general and specific exercises. (Additional types like weight loss % or point systems can be added in later phases.)
	•	Challenge Format: Individual (everyone competes individually on one leaderboard) or Teams (users split into teams that compete collectively). Priority: MVP can launch with individual format only, and add team support in a subsequent release.
	•	Duration: Start date and end date. For MVP, allow preset durations (e.g. 1 week, 2 weeks, 1 month, 3 months) and a custom range option. Ensure challenges can be future-dated (so you can set up a challenge to start next Monday, etc.).
	•	Participant Limit: Optionally set a max number of participants (or unlimited). This ties into pricing – e.g. challenges up to X people might be free or a base fee, larger ones higher fee.
	•	Privacy: Public (anyone with link or browsing public challenges can join) vs Private (invite only). MVP can start with invite-only by link; public listing can come later once community grows.
	•	Title and Description: Custom name (e.g. “Spring Step Challenge”) and a description/rules field (so organizers can specify any rules or fun context).
	•	Enrollment Fee (if any, optional): Not to be confused with the app’s challenge fee – this is if the organizer wants each participant to pay to join (for prize pool or charity). This is a stretch feature (not MVP) inspired by StepBet, but initially we will not handle money pools – focus is on one-time fee paid by organizer to app, not between users.
	•	Challenge Dashboard: Once created, a challenge has a dashboard screen that shows:
	•	Challenge Info: name, duration (countdown or time remaining), number of participants, maybe an icon or cover image.
	•	Leaderboard: Real-time ranking of participants (or teams) based on the chosen metric ￼. Show each participant’s total and perhaps daily breakdown. Support toggling views (e.g. in a 4-week challenge, view by total or by week). The leaderboard updates whenever new data comes in from sync (with an indicator like “last updated 5 min ago”).
	•	User’s Stats: Highlight the logged-in user’s current progress (e.g. “You are 3rd with 40,000 steps” and how far behind 2nd place).
	•	Activity Feed (stretch): A scrollable feed of recent activities or milestones – e.g. “Alice logged 10,000 steps today” or “Brian completed a 5km run”. For MVP, this could be a simple event log, and evolve into a richer social feed with comments or photos (like GymRats style) later.
	•	Invite Status: For organizers, a section to invite more people or see pending invites; for participants, a way to suggest the challenge to others (sharing link).
	•	Invite/Join via Link: Each challenge generates a unique invite link or code. Users can share this via any channel (messaging, social media, email). Clicking the link:
	•	If user has the app installed, it deep-links to the challenge join screen.
	•	If not installed, it takes them to the App Store/Play Store, and after installation, ideally the app detects the invite and prompts to join that challenge (using Expo’s linking and Supabase deep link capabilities).
	•	Alternatively, a user can enter an invite code within the app.
	•	Joining flow: New users will be prompted to sign up (or log in) then land at the challenge join confirmation. Existing users just confirm joining. If the challenge has a participant limit and is full, they’d get a message it’s full (and possibly a waitlist if we implement that).
	•	We will not require being friends on the app to join a challenge – the link bypasses that, aligning with ease-of-sharing principles seen in Challenges app ￼ and Stridekick.
	•	Challenge Settings & Admin: The challenge creator is the admin by default. Admin capabilities:
	•	Approve or remove participants (for private challenges, if we implement a request-join model in future).
	•	Remove any inappropriate content in the feed (if feed exists).
	•	End the challenge early or extend duration (MVP can omit this for simplicity; challenges run their course once started).
	•	For team challenges: assign people to teams or auto-balance teams.
	•	Manage fees: if one-time challenge fee needs to be paid, the admin (creator) will be prompted at creation to pay (see Pricing section).
	•	Possibly designate co-admins (not MVP).
	•	Notifications: Participants get push notifications (and/or email) for important events:
	•	Challenge starting soon (e.g. 24 hours before start, “Get ready!”).
	•	Challenge ended and results (who won, or personal result).
	•	Daily/weekly progress notifications (“You’re in 2nd place today!” or “Alice just overtook you in the challenge” – need to throttle so it’s motivating not spammy).
	•	If someone comments or cheers (when that feature exists).
	•	Invites: if you’re invited to a challenge, you get a notification to join.

2. Fitness Data Sync & Tracking
	•	Integrations (MVP): The app will integrate with key fitness data sources to automatically fetch workout/activity data:
	•	Apple HealthKit (iOS): Allows reading relevant data types like step count, distance walked/run, active energy (calories), exercise minutes, and specific workouts. Upon sign-up or first use, iOS users will be prompted to connect Apple Health and grant permissions. This will let the app pull past days’ data and subscribe to updates. (Supabase can store access tokens or we can use Expo’s HealthKit APIs to read data locally then send to our DB.)
	•	Google Fit & Health Connect (Android): For Android, we’ll use Google’s Health Connect (or Fit API if needed) to get similar data (steps, distance, calories, workout sessions). The user will connect their Google account or give Health Connect permission. This covers data from many sources on Android (Samsung Health, etc., via Health Connect) ￼ ￼.
	•	Third-Party API Integrations: In addition to phone-based data, allow linking accounts for Fitbit and Garmin in MVP if feasible. Fitbit’s Web API and Garmin’s API would require OAuth flows:
	•	Fitbit: User logs in via OAuth, we fetch daily steps, activities. This is crucial for users who rely on Fitbit and don’t carry their phone for steps.
	•	Garmin: Garmin has an API but might be harder for a small app (possible use through a service or manual upload). If direct integration is complex for MVP, we might rely on those users linking Garmin->Apple Health or Garmin->Google Fit (Garmin can sync to Apple Health for iOS, and now via Health Connect on Android) ￼. However, direct Garmin integration is a plus for enthusiast appeal.
	•	Strava API (optional stretch): Strava has an OAuth API to fetch logged activities (runs, rides). We could include this especially for challenges based on distance or specific workouts, since many people record their runs on Strava. But note, Strava data might overlap with Apple/Google since those can have the same workouts – we need to intelligently avoid double counting. Possibly let user choose their preferred source.
	•	In summary, MVP integration priority: Apple Health and Google Fit (for broad coverage), plus Fitbit (as it’s a large user base). Garmin/Strava can follow in later updates unless resources allow early inclusion.
	•	Manual Entry (fallback): For completeness, allow users to manually log an activity in the app in case their device isn’t syncing. E.g. if someone doesn’t have any tracker, they can enter “30 minute run” or “5000 steps” manually. This entry would update their challenge stats. However, manual entry could be prone to cheating; we might mark manually entered data in the feed for transparency. MVP could omit manual entry to encourage integration use, but to not exclude users with no device, a simple step entry might be allowed (with perhaps a cap or verification step). This is similar to Stridekick’s approach, which allows manual steps input but the fun is in automatic tracking ￼.
	•	Data Sync Frequency: The app will periodically sync data in the background. For steps and ongoing counters, we might poll HealthKit/GoogleFit every few minutes or use background update triggers. Supabase will store user’s daily stats and aggregate per challenge. We will design so that even if a user doesn’t open the app, their steps/workouts still count (background fetch and push notifications will handle this). Participants can also pull-to-refresh their challenge screen to trigger an immediate sync.
	•	Workout Categorization: We will classify incoming workout data by type (run, walk, swim, etc.) to allow future specific workout challenges. For MVP, general metrics (steps, minutes, etc.) use aggregate data. But our data model should store individual workout entries too. E.g., if a challenge is “Most swimming distance”, we need to filter Apple Health workouts for Swimming only. Thus, from the start, our integration code will tag data with workout type. Challenges of type “specific workout” will use those filters (not MVP, but plan for it).
	•	Accuracy & Cheat Prevention: Because we integrate official sources, data should be as accurate as those devices provide. To discourage cheating:
	•	If manual entry is allowed, consider limits or requiring photo proof (though that’s more friction). Possibly for MVP, lean on trust and social pressure – friends will notice if someone logs unrealistic entries.
	•	We can implement server-side checks (e.g. no more than 100k steps a day allowed, etc.) based on human limits.
	•	For competitive scenarios, having integration means most will use their trackers which are hard to fake without considerable effort (which casual users likely won’t).
	•	Multi-Source Data Merge: Some users may connect multiple sources (e.g. Apple Health and Fitbit). We should establish a priority or merging logic to avoid double counting. For instance: If Apple Health is pulling steps from a Fitbit device (which can happen via sync), and the user also connected Fitbit directly, we might get duplicate data. A solution: Let the user select their primary step source at setup (e.g. “Prefer data from: [Apple Health / Fitbit / Garmin]”). Or detect overlaps by timestamps/IDs and de-duplicate. This could be complex, so a simpler MVP route: encourage one source per user for each metric (the linking UI can advise “If you have a wearable, connect that and disable phone counting, or vice versa”).

3. Social & Engagement Features
	•	User Profiles: Basic profile for each user with a display name, avatar, and connected accounts summary (e.g. icons indicating if they have a Fitbit linked, etc.). Not a social media profile, but so challenge mates can identify each other. Possibly include a short bio or fitness quote for fun. Privacy settings to allow pseudonyms if desired (some corporate users might prefer anonymity in public challenges).
	•	Friends & Following (Later): FitGrit v1 does not need a full social network of friends – challenge-centric means you interact within each challenge. However, to facilitate future re-engagement, we plan a “friends list” or at least an option to “re-invite past participants” in new challenges. This acts like a lightweight social graph. MVP can simply allow adding people by username to invite (besides links), which implicitly builds a friend list.
	•	Challenge Feed & Chat: Within a challenge, having communication is key to group motivation. MVP will include a challenge chat feature:
	•	A text chat thread all members can participate in. They can send messages like encouragement, tips, or playful banter (similar to Fitbit challenges’ banter and Challenges app’s wall ￼).
	•	Possibly allow reactions or quick emojis on the leaderboard (e.g. tap a user and “high-five” them).
	•	Content moderation: provide ability for users to report or for admin to delete messages. Keep it simple (and rely on community guidelines).
	•	No need for 1-1 messaging at this stage, just group chat per challenge.
	•	Milestone Celebrations: To keep things fun, the app can automatically highlight certain milestones in the challenge feed or via notifications. E.g. “🎉 John just hit 50,000 steps!” or “All team members have logged at least one workout this week”. These little celebratory messages can boost engagement (we’ll make them configurable for frequency).
	•	Badges & Awards: At challenge completion, award virtual badges/trophies:
	•	e.g. Winner badge for 1st place, Finisher badge for meeting a certain goal (if the challenge had an explicit goal, like everyone aim to do 10 workouts – those who did get a badge), Consistency badge (for meeting daily targets, etc.).
	•	Badges appear on the user’s profile or a “trophy case”. These provide long-term motivation and bragging rights.
	•	MVP could simply mark the winner in the app and have a basic badge graphic. More elaborate badge systems can come later.
	•	Optionally, allow the organizer to input a prize (like “Winner gets a gift card” – but actual prize distribution is outside app’s scope; this would just be noted in description).
	•	Public Challenges Directory (Future): Down the line, we may have a discovery section where users can find open challenges to join (like a monthly global step challenge). In MVP, focus on invite-based joining. Later, add a “Browse Challenges” (particularly for the solo challenger archetype) which can drive engagement when a user’s friends aren’t currently doing one.

4. User Sign-Up / Login and Onboarding
	•	Sign-Up/Login: Use Supabase Auth to handle user accounts. Support standard email/password sign-up and popular OAuth options: Sign in with Apple, Google, maybe Facebook (though Apple & Google likely suffice given our audience and easier integration with Expo) ￼. The recent GymRats update points out the convenience of social and magic link logins ￼ – we can similarly implement magic link login via email for simplicity (Supabase supports magic links).
	•	Onboarding Flow:
	1.	New user opens app via a challenge invite link or directly.
	2.	If via invite, after account creation, auto-join prompt for that challenge.
	3.	Prompt user to connect a fitness data source:
	•	On iOS: show HealthKit permission request.
	•	On Android: request Google Fit/Health Connect permission or sign into Google account.
	•	Offer alternative “Connect your device” options: Fitbit or others. Possibly show logos of Apple, Google, Fitbit, Garmin and let user tap those to connect. This step can be skipped and done later, but we will stress that connecting will enhance their experience (auto-tracking).
	4.	Perhaps a brief tutorial: one or two screens explaining how to create or join challenges, where to find your stats. Keep it minimal since we want to get them into the action quickly, especially if they came from an invite (they likely already have context from a friend).
	•	Sign-Up Incentives: We may consider allowing users to join one challenge without creating an account fully (guest join with minimal info) to reduce friction, but since data sync is critical, having an account is likely required. Instead, we make sign-up quick with social logins and not ask for too many details.
	•	Security & Privacy: All standard practices (secure password storage via Supabase, option to delete account, etc.). Privacy settings allow a user to set their profile visibility (but since challenges are semi-private groups by nature, profile info is mostly just seen by challenge mates). Ensure we handle health data responsibly – only use the minimum scope needed and explain to users what data we collect (steps, workouts, etc.).

5. Cross-Platform and Technical Considerations
	•	The app is built in React Native (Expo), meaning we maintain a single codebase for iOS and Android. This ensures feature parity and simultaneous rollout on both platforms, fulfilling the cross-platform requirement. Expo provides easy integration for HealthKit (via expo-health or community libraries) and potentially for Google Fit (via Google’s APIs or Health Connect bridging).
	•	Supabase Backend: We use Supabase (which provides Postgres DB, Auth, and real-time subscriptions). Key usage:
	•	Store user profiles and linked account tokens (Fitbit tokens, etc).
	•	Store challenge definitions and membership.
	•	Store activity data (or at least daily aggregates per user) that’s pulled from sources. Possibly maintain a table of raw workouts/steps to allow flexible queries (e.g. summing by type).
	•	Use Supabase Realtime or listen using subscriptions to update challenge leaderboards live when data changes.
	•	Auth as mentioned for login.
	•	Supabase also allows edge functions; we could use those for certain server-side computations (like cron jobs to finalize challenges, send summary emails, etc.).
	•	Scalability: From day one, design to handle challenges with thousands of participants. This affects:
	•	Data model: For example, writing a row per step may not scale; better to aggregate daily per user per challenge. But we also want granularity for some challenges (like counting distinct workouts). A balance: store each workout session, but for steps which are continuous, maybe just store daily total. Leaderboard calculation can then sum those quickly.
	•	UI: If 5000 people in a challenge, the leaderboard needs paging or at least showing top 50 and your rank (with maybe few above/below you). We’ll implement lazy loading of leaderboard entries and a search function to find yourself or others.
	•	Real-time updates for 1000+ users could be heavy; we might limit the frequency of live updates (maybe update leaderboards every X minutes or on pull-to-refresh for very large challenges, instead of every second).
	•	Testing with large dummy data to ensure performance on the frontend and backend is crucial.
	•	Platform Features: Ensure support for things like push notifications on both platforms (Expo’s Notifications API), deep linking (Expo Linking), and background fetch (to update data periodically, which Expo supports with background tasks).
	•	Offline support: Basic offline capability where the last known leaderboard is visible and a message “offline, will sync when connected” – not a huge focus but the app should degrade gracefully if someone has spotty connection on a run.

Prioritized Feature Roadmap

We will prioritize features into MVP (Must-have for initial launch), v1.1 (Next priority after launch), and Future Enhancements. This ensures we deliver core value first and then iterate based on user feedback and strategic goals.

MVP – Must Have Features:
	1.	Account Creation & Login: Email/Password and OAuth (Apple/Google) sign-in ￼. Basic profile setup.
	2.	Create/Join Challenge (Individual format): Ability to create a challenge with type (steps, distance, etc.), set duration, invite via link. Join flow via link or code – core user story.
	3.	Automatic Data Sync (Apple Health & Google Fit): Connect to Apple HealthKit (iOS) and Google Fit/Health Connect (Android) to fetch steps, active minutes, and distance. Use this data to update user’s progress in challenges.
	4.	Basic Leaderboard: Display participant rankings with their progress towards the challenge metric ￼. Update periodically.
	5.	Invite Links: Generate and handle deep links to invite new users to challenges easily ￼.
	6.	Notifications: Push notifications for challenge invites and start/end of challenge.
	7.	Challenge Chat: Simple group chat or comment wall within each challenge for participant interaction ￼.
	8.	One-Time Fee Payment Flow: Implement in-app purchase or payment collection for challenge creation based on size:
	•	Likely use App Store/Play Store in-app purchases (consumables) or Stripe integration via Supabase if allowed. For MVP, a simpler approach: allow X small challenges free to seed usage, then implement fees by v1.1 if needed (this depends on strategy – we may want to test engagement before turning on payments).
	•	But the requirement is clear on one-time fee, so MVP should at least have a mechanism to handle a purchase when creating a challenge that exceeds the free limit (e.g. free for up to 5 people, otherwise pay). This can be tested in closed beta.
	9.	Cross-Platform Release: The app should launch on both iOS and Android simultaneously to fulfill its promise.
	10.	Basic Analytics & Logging: (For internal use) Track active users, challenge creations, etc., to monitor performance and usage.

Post-MVP (v1.1 and beyond) – High Priority:
	1.	More Integrations: Add Fitbit integration ￼ and possibly Garmin. This will broaden the user base and appeal to those device owners. Fitbit likely high priority given popularity.
	2.	Team Challenges: Enable creating team-based challenges (with the app handling team score aggregation and team leaderboards). This appeals to corporate and large friend groups.
	3.	Additional Challenge Types: E.g. support weight loss (% change) challenges, or multi-metric ones (like a triathlon challenge combining swim/bike/run distances). Weight/BMI challenges would need manual input or smart scale integration. We can start with something like “Workout Streak challenge” (who can log activity every day).
	4.	Public Challenges & Exploration: A section to join global or public challenges, turning the app into a community hub for those who want ongoing challenges even without friend invites.
	5.	Friends List & Social Graph: Let users add friends and see which challenges they’re in or easily invite them without link. Possibly allow following people.
	6.	Enhance Notifications & Engagement: More granular notifications settings (user can opt in to daily progress reminders, etc.), email summaries for completed challenges with results (and perhaps shareable graphics).
	7.	Monetization Tweaks: Evaluate the one-time fee model based on usage. Possibly introduce a tier where small challenges are free and larger ones require payment, as originally planned. Or a wallet system for organizers who frequently pay. Also consider partnerships (sponsored challenges) for revenue without charging users heavily.
	8.	Web Dashboard: Particularly for corporate organizers, a web interface to set up and monitor challenges could be valuable (though this could be a longer-term item, given we chose React Native for mobile focus).
	9.	Gamification & Badges: Expand the badges, add levels or points for participating in challenges to increase retention (a user leveling system that persists beyond a single challenge).
	10.	Localization & Accessibility: Ensure the app can be used by a wider audience (multilingual support if expanding beyond US, and accessibility features like VoiceOver compliance).

Future Enhancements (Longer-term ideas):
	•	Integration with Wearables directly: e.g. an Apple Watch companion app for quick check of leaderboard, or integration with Android Wear OS tiles.
	•	Charity Challenges: Partner with charities so that the one-time fees or user contributions can go to a cause, making challenges more meaningful (similar to Charity Miles concept) – could attract more users to pay for challenges.
	•	AI Coaching Element: Use the data from challenges to provide insights or tips (“You tend to slow down mid-week. Try scheduling a group run on Wednesdays!”) – not core to challenges but value-add.
	•	Marketplace for Challenges: Allow users to create challenge templates and share them. E.g., a fitness influencer could host paid entry challenges that their followers join (with perhaps a prize). The app could support these with built-in payment distribution, though this introduces complexity (that edges into StepBet territory with real money prizes).

User Experience & Flow

We describe a few primary user flows to illustrate how a user will interact with FitGrit:

Flow 1: Create and Share a New Challenge (Small Friend Group)
	1.	Home Screen: After login, the user sees a “Create Challenge” button (and any active or upcoming challenges listed).
	2.	Create Challenge Wizard: Alice taps “Create Challenge”. She selects “Total Steps Challenge”. She sets it to start immediately (today) and last 2 weeks. She leaves it as individual competition. She sets the name “March Step Madness” and continues.
	3.	Invite Step: The app shows “Invite friends to join your challenge” with options: copy invite link, share to WhatsApp/Email/etc directly. Alice taps to copy the link and maybe also chooses friends from a suggested list (say, Bob who was in her last challenge is suggested).
	4.	Payment Step: Because her challenge is under 10 people (she anticipates 5 friends), and our model says up to 5 is free (for example), it says “Free challenge – no fee required” or if it was larger, it would prompt “This challenge will cost $2.99 – [Purchase]”. (Assume free in this small case.) She finalizes creation.
	5.	Challenge Lobby: The challenge screen is now visible to Alice (currently only she is in it, shown on leaderboard with 0 steps). It says “Waiting for others to join” with a hint to remind her friends.
	6.	Friends Join: Alice pastes the invite link to her group chat. Bob (iPhone user with Apple Watch) clicks it, it opens the App Store (since he didn’t have the app). He installs, signs up quickly with Apple ID. After onboarding, the app automatically shows “You’ve been invited to March Step Madness – Join now?” Bob accepts, and is now in the challenge.
	•	Bob is prompted to connect Apple Health; he does, granting steps access. The app pulls Bob’s steps for today (say 3,000 steps so far) and updates the challenge leaderboard.
	•	Alice sees Bob appear on the leaderboard with 3,000 steps, while she has maybe 4,000 from her morning walk (pulled from HealthKit on her phone).
	7.	Real-Time Competition: As others join (some on Android connecting Google Fit, some on Fitbit linking that account), the leaderboard fills out. Throughout the 2 weeks, each person’s steps update periodically. Alice opens the app each evening to check ranks; if she’s behind, it motivates her to take an extra walk the next day.
	•	The chat sees some messages: “Bob: Good luck everyone!” and mid-way “Charlie: I’m coming for that #1 spot 😅!”.
	•	The app sends a mid-challenge notification: “1 week left in March Step Madness – Alice is in the lead with 50,000 steps!”
	8.	Challenge Completion: At the end date, the challenge automatically closes. The final leaderboard freezes. The app crowns the winner: e.g. “Alice wins! 🏆 102,000 steps”. Everyone gets a notification of results.
	•	Alice can tap “Share results” which creates a graphic of the leaderboard or her achievement to share on social media (potential viral growth vector).
	•	A badge “March Step Madness Winner” is added to Alice’s profile. Others get a “Challenger” badge for completing.
	•	The group chat remains so they can congratulate and perhaps discuss the next challenge (the app could prompt “Re-match? Create a new challenge with the same group”).

Flow 2: Join an Existing Challenge via Link (First-time user)
	1.	David receives a link from a colleague for a “Workweek Workout Challenge” (goal: at least 30 active minutes each day, Mon-Fri). He’s never used FitGrit.
	2.	Clicking the link opens the Play Store (he’s on Android). He installs FitGrit.
	3.	On first open, the app recognizes the deep link and says “You’re invited to join ‘Acme Corp Wellness Challenge’.” He taps Sign Up, uses his Google account for quick sign-in.
	4.	Next, it asks to connect a data source. David has a Samsung watch which syncs to Samsung Health, but since we integrated Health Connect, we instruct: “Connect to Health Connect to track your activities.” He grants permission. The app finds his active minutes for the day (say he walked 20 min at lunch).
	5.	It then auto-joins him into the challenge. He sees the challenge details and that 50 coworkers are participating. The leaderboard might be sorted by how many days they met 30 min so far (depending on challenge rule). He’s a bit behind since he joined day 2, but he can catch up.
	6.	David posts a message in the chat, “Hi all, excited to join!” and gets welcomes. This quick join process (link -> sign in -> data connect -> in challenge) took only a couple of minutes.
	7.	For payment: since this is presumably a corporate challenge, maybe the company already paid a fee for a large group when it was created, so David isn’t prompted to pay anything. (If this was a scenario where each user pays individually – which we decided not to do in our model – we’d handle it differently. Our model charges the creator up front, not participants.)

Flow 3: Data Sync and Activity Logging
	1.	Emma is in a running distance challenge (target: most miles in October). She uses Strava to record runs on her phone.
	2.	During onboarding, Emma connected Apple Health, and her Strava app writes runs to HealthKit. After her morning run of 5 miles, Strava logs it to HealthKit.
	3.	FitGrit’s background task or push from HealthKit detects a new workout. The app retrieves the workout (5 miles running) and updates Emma’s total in the challenge.
	4.	Emma receives a push: “Great run! You’ve logged 5 miles towards October Run Challenge.” When she checks the app later, she sees her distance added and that she moved from 5th to 3rd place on the leaderboard.
	5.	Another participant, Frank, doesn’t use a fancy app – he just carries his phone. He gave FitGrit Google Fit access, so it counts his steps and estimates distance. His contributions update similarly (less precise for distance than Emma’s GPS data, but it’s something).
	6.	If Emma also had a Fitbit and connected it, the app would need to avoid counting the same run twice (we handle that by prioritizing Apple Health and not double-counting Fitbit data if HealthKit already has it).
	7.	Throughout the challenge, FitGrit continues to sync periodically. Users can also open the app and pull-to-refresh if they want the latest data immediately.

Flow 4: Paying for a Challenge (Monetization)
	1.	Carlos wants to create a big public challenge for his 1000 Twitter followers – “100K Steps Weekend”. He creates the challenge, sets it public, max 1000 people.
	2.	The app shows: “Large Challenge Fee: $9.99 – This covers up to 1000 participants for this challenge. [Confirm and Pay]”. (This is an example pricing; actual might scale tiers.)
	3.	Carlos pays via in-app purchase (fingerprint/Face ID). The challenge is created.
	4.	He shares the link on Twitter. People join. Because he paid the fee, joiners are not charged anything. If the challenge hits capacity (1000), no more can join (or if we allow, maybe that would require an upgrade fee).
	5.	The app keeps track of participant count; if by some chance the challenge doesn’t attract as many, that’s fine (fee isn’t refunded, it was capacity-based not per head).
	6.	This flow demonstrates the one-time fee per challenge concept – smooth for the participants (they see no paywalls) and the cost is handled by the organizer as part of setup.

Non-Functional Requirements
	•	Performance: The app should load the challenge leaderboard within 2-3 seconds even with large participant counts (using lazy loading if needed). Data sync operations should be efficient and mostly in background.
	•	Reliability: Sync jobs should be resilient – e.g., if Fitbit’s API fails at a moment, app should retry later. Supabase backend should handle concurrent writes (we may use row-level locking or upsert logic for aggregating step counts to avoid race conditions when many updates come in).
	•	Security: Protect user data (especially health data) – use secure authentication tokens, do not unnecessarily store raw sensitive data. All communications encrypted (HTTPS). Follow platform rules for handling health data (e.g. iOS requires a purpose string for Health data usage, and not using it beyond stated purposes).
	•	Scalability: Design database schema to handle growth: e.g., separate tables for challenge membership, daily stats, etc., with proper indexing. Use Supabase’s ability to horizontally scale the DB if needed. Also consider using caching for leaderboards (maybe compute standings periodically rather than on every request if scale demands).
	•	Compatibility: Support the last couple of major OS versions on iOS (iOS 15+) and Android (Android 9/10+). Expo will handle much of this as it uses native capabilities.
	•	Testing: Thoroughly test with different device scenarios (Apple Watch user, Fitbit user, phone-only user) to ensure data is captured correctly. Also test the invite flows across platforms (deep link from iOS to Android, etc.). For large challenges, simulate many entries to see performance.

Conclusion

FitGrit aims to combine the strengths observed in the competitive analysis: Strava’s broad device support and social feed, Fitbit’s friendly competition, Apple’s polished fitness metrics, and niche apps’ focus on ease of challenge creation and cross-platform play. By focusing on fitness challenges as the core experience, FitGrit will avoid feature bloat (no endless social feed, no complex diet trackers – unless we integrate a simple Eat Well challenge mode later). This clarity of purpose will reflect in the UI/UX: users open the app and immediately engage with their challenges – checking progress, entering a new one, or creating their next competition.

The one-time fee monetization aligns with how users naturally organize fitness bets or events (one-off, time-bound). It avoids the fatigue of yet another subscription ￼, while still providing a revenue stream when the app’s popularity grows and big communities partake. We will carefully monitor user reception to this model; if small-scale personal use needs to be free to compete with alternatives, we ensure at least basic usage is free and only larger scale or special features incur fees.

In summary, FitGrit’s PRD lays out a comprehensive yet focused plan to deliver a cross-platform, challenge-centric fitness app. By learning from and improving upon existing solutions – offering flexible integrations, easy sharing, and a clear pay-per-challenge model – FitGrit is positioned to carve out a strong niche in the fitness app market, turning every workout into an opportunity for friendly competition and group achievement.
